Background

Monthly values in this rose

Where this air was caught

Every week, at remote coastlines and islands around the world, partners of NOAA's Global Greenhouse Gas Reference Network fill glass flasks with air and send them to the Global Monitoring Laboratory in Boulder, Colorado. There, every sample is measured on the same instruments.12

The global numbers in these roses come from a chosen subset of those places: marine boundary layer sites, remote and at sea level, where the wind comes in off the ocean and one flask stands for a vast volume of well-mixed air.3 For carbon dioxide that is 42 sites. NOAA fits each week's measurements into a smooth curve from pole to pole and averages it by latitude.4

The whiskers in the opened rose are NOAA's uncertainty. Much of it comes from asking how the answer would change if a different set of sites had been sampled, a bootstrap test NOAA runs on its own network.4 For carbon dioxide in 2025 it averaged ±0.12 ppm, some 36 times smaller than that year's seasonal swing. The newest months carry no whiskers yet: they stay preliminary until the last flasks come home and are measured.

Adding up the warming

What comes next

NASA's analysis puts October 2026 at 1.55 °C above its 1951–1980 average, the largest monthly anomaly in a record that reaches back to 1880.8 A strong El Niño is building in the Pacific, and NOAA's forecasters expect a strong to very strong event to last into early 2027.6 The steepest carbon dioxide rises in this record came in El Niño years, and the World Meteorological Organization warns this one could push concentrations higher still.7

Each new ring on these roses will be drawn a little further out.
